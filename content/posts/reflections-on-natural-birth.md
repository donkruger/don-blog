I had one simple goal for my speech at Richard’s wedding: don’t bore people.

So, I decided to tell the story of the four of us. Me, Michaela, the bride, and the groom, spending four days in the wild hiking the Drakensberg Mountains.

It was a long story, but one worth telling. The best you can hope for when giving a speech of such a personal calibre on an intimate occasion is a few genuine chuckles and an engaged room. A good speech, I discovered, is mostly a question of cadence: knowing when to rush, when to slow down, and when to let it breathe…

Micky was sitting in the crowd, nine months pregnant.

Despite the unexpected nerves and an uninvited tear I shed towards the end of my speech, I’d call it an overall win. Watching one of my dearest friends marry the love of his life was a privilege. Shortly after, he and his bride headed back into that same timeless Drakensberg range for their honeymoon. I wish them nothing but a long, happy, and healthy marriage.

Exactly two days after the wedding, my son, Ashton James Kruger, was born (now nicknamed Ash). I like to think he waited politely for my best man speech to be delivered before making his arrival on planet Earth.

From a wedding to a birth in forty-eight hours, and through the weeks that have followed since, my mind has kept returning to the sheer physical miracle of how Ash arrived. Watching Michaela bring our son into the world without conventional pain medication changed how I think about human resilience, the natural rhythm of our bodies, and what we lose when modern medicine tries to hurry or bypass a process that took millions of years to perfect.

## Natural Labour and the Courage to Endure It

If you want to develop a new reverence for human resilience, watch a woman go through natural labour without an epidural.

Michaela was extraordinary.

We live in a medical environment with a heavy proclivity for scheduled interventions. That is especially true in South African private hospitals, where elective C-sections have practically become the default path, and it is increasingly true abroad. A recent investigation in The Times explored how Britain has become the "C-section capital of the West," with rates doubling in a decade as clinicians warn of a culture driven by fear and defensive medicine. It is a trend that is taking hold in many countries. Even in South Africa, because it is now so rare for women to opt for an unmedicated natural delivery, I could sense the maternity ward nurses responding to Michaela with a distinct, unspoken level of respect.

Watching what she went through solidified an opinion that might be unpopular in our hyper-medicalised era, but one I believe deeply: more women need to find the bravery to tackle natural birth, and our medical system needs to stop talking them out of it.

In our rush to sanitise, schedule, and de-risk childbirth, excessive medical intervention is often making things worse rather than better. When we treat a healthy pregnancy as a clinical pathology waiting to go wrong, we rob mothers and babies of an evolutionary process refined over millions of years. My opinion is understandably upsetting to many (especially mothers who have already gone the C-section route), but I’d appreciate it if you would hear me out…

Labour, I learnt, keeps its own cadence. Contractions arrive in waves that build, peak, and release, and the rest between them is as much a part of the work as the effort itself. It helped me to think of the nine-month pregnancy not as a waiting room where labour suddenly switches on at the forty-week mark, but as a gradual, nine-month ramp-up in intensity. Micky’s aches and pains steadily built toward the climactic effort of bringing new life into the world. It cannot be scripted on a hospital timetable. It can only be listened to.

That is not to say the process was dignified and quiet. Her screams ran right up and down the hallways of the birth ward.

At one point, Ashton’s heart rate dipped, and the doctor wanted to take Michaela to theatre for a C-section. It was a serious moment and a textbook example of how quickly defensive medicine pulls the surgical trigger.

When you are on the clock in a risk-averse system, surgery is the easiest lever to pull. Fortunately, we had two incredible midwives, Belinda and Wikje, beside her. At the time, I felt they were nothing short of angels standing at the forefront of bringing life into this world the way our bodies evolved to. While the medical team monitored Ash closely, they helped Micky hold her conviction all the way to the finish line.

Had a C-section become genuinely necessary to save my son or my wife, I would have been grateful the theatre was down the hall. But Michaela was able to continue, and I am fiercely proud of what she and Ashton accomplished together.

Later, I ducked out of the labour room for a few seconds to grab some supplies from the room where she had laboured through the early stages. Stepping into the corridor, I walked past two very calm women with relaxed smiles on their faces, waiting to be wheeled into theatre for their scheduled C-sections. They looked at me, listened to Michaela’s scream echoing down the passage behind me, and gave me a silent, sympathetic "good luck" look.

The contrast struck me. Choosing the unmedicated path when a pain-free, scheduled exit door is right down the corridor requires unfathomable bravery. Yet that bravery pays dividends that modern convenience cannot replicate.

One of our primary reasons for insisting on a natural birth is a remarkable piece of physiology in a baby’s transition to breathing. The sheer physical compression of being pushed through the birth canal (ranked among the most painful experiences a human can endure) squeezes the amniotic fluid out of the baby’s lungs and primes their respiratory and immune systems for the outside world. Some steps cannot be skipped, and no amount of surgical urgency can mimic them. Ashton breathed (and cried) with full force from his first few seconds out in the world. Having been born by C-section myself, I do suspect my own childhood asthma was the downstream cost of bypassing that natural compression (a link backed by a growing body of medical research on C-section deliveries and respiratory outcomes).

My opinion on the matter intensifies a bit after having heard several horror stories from postpartum C-section mommies. We rarely talk about what we steal from a mother’s neurochemistry when we turn birth into a passive surgical procedure.

Enduring and conquering the intensity of natural labour triggers a profound, evolutionary cascade of hormones (oxytocin, endorphins, and adrenaline) designed to wire a mother’s brain for immediate bonding and resilience. Crossing that finish line under her own power gives a woman an unshakeable sense of agency.

Bypassing that natural rhythm through unnecessary intervention leaves too many women recovering from major abdominal surgery while feeling disconnected from the birth itself, making them far more vulnerable to postpartum depression.

There is also the sheer cocktail of drugs pumped into a woman’s body during heavily medicated and surgical deliveries. Those anaesthetics, synthetic hormones, and heavy painkillers do not stay isolated to the mother; they cross over to the child. You see it in how many babies come out completely loopy, groggy, and sluggish in their first hours of life, fighting through a chemical fog at the exact moment they are meant to be alert, latching, and bonding with their mothers.

When medical intervention becomes the default rather than the rescue mechanism, it creates the very complications it claims to prevent. Fear-based, defensive obstetrics interrupts the body’s natural rhythm, stalls labour, induces panic, and manufactures emergencies that end on an operating table.

And the pharmacological conveyor belt does not stop once the baby is delivered. The heavy rotation of postoperative drugs, painkillers, and sedatives that follow in the wake of surgical intervention dulls a mother’s physical and mental resilience right when she needs her vitality most. By blunting her body's natural hormonal recovery and replacing it with a chemical one, the process leaves mothers emotionally flatlined and significantly more susceptible to postpartum psychological complications and depression.

We experienced that same medical reflex toward chemical shortcuts firsthand even after a natural delivery. When we voiced a concern that Michaela’s breast milk had not fully come in yet, the immediate response was a proposal for milk-inducing hormone medication, offered with almost no discussion about trusting her body’s timing or the negative side effects of artificially forcing lactation. I am grateful we held off and researched it ourselves. For one, it is completely normal for mature milk to take a few days to arrive. More importantly, that delay is evolutionary by design: what arrives first is a concentrated, nutrient-dense colostrum that seeds the foundation of a baby’s gut microbiome. That initial window before full milk production also triggers the newborn’s ketone production and ketosis mechanisms, switching on vital metabolic survival pathways for later in life. Rushing to medicate the mother or throwing a baby straight onto formula out of impatience robs them of that foundational biological calibration.

At its core, this comes down to how we view doing hard things in life. Think about the bravery it takes to run a marathon and the profound sense of achievement that washes over you when you cross that finish line. Nobody runs 42 kilometres because it is painless or convenient; you do it because enduring the agony and pushing through the wall transforms you. You would never tell a marathon runner to take a taxi to the finish line just to spare them the sweat and discomfort. We need to start training mothers to think about childbirth the same way: to prepare their minds and bodies to train for labour, to embrace the grit it demands, and to be immensely proud of themselves when they conquer it naturally.

Is there lots of screaming? Hell yes. Should women be afraid of it? I think fear is not the necessary response, but a respect for the natural process and the natural rewards needs to be more prevalent I feel. I imagine the entire maternity ward at Netcare Pretoria East Hospital must have been slightly on edge for a few hours while Michaela fought through her 7.5-hour battle, or perhaps they are used to it. Either way, she was unfathomably brave.

It is worth mentioning that being there also gave me a deep appreciation for the people who keep a maternity ward running around the clock. Doctors, nurses, and midwives moved through a relentless 24/7 system of monitoring, decisions, handovers, and care. Shift after shift, handover after handover, a human system built to hold its rhythm through day and night. It made me appreciate not just the practice of the operations, but the orchestration of the humans behind it.

## Three Billion Heartbeats

I am still weighing the good and the bad of the hospital experience as a whole. Modern hospitals are strange, clinical places to welcome a new human being, and I wish their designers cared a little more about warmth, softer lighting, and putting a living plant or two in the corner of the room.

Still, I am not blind to the privilege of the safety net around us. Across the animal kingdom, most mammals get roughly one billion heartbeats in a lifetime, whether they burn through them in two years like a mouse or stretch them across decades like a whale. Humans are the great exception, routinely reaching three billion heartbeats. We earned those extra two billion beats through centuries of patient human innovation in sanitation, science, and genuine emergency medicine.

The mistake of modern obstetrics is forgetting when to use that power and when to stand down. True medicine is there to rescue a life when nature falters, not to script a life before it has even begun.

Watching Michaela bring our son into the world taught me that life itself demands that same respect. You cannot rush a four-day hike through the Drakensberg, you cannot skip the hard miles of a marathon, and you should not shortcut the ancient, brutal, and extraordinary work of birth.

Ashton James Kruger is here now, healthy, alert, and right at the beginning of his three billion heartbeats. Watching his mother fight for every single second of his arrival was among the greatest privileges of my life.
