I had one simple goal for my speech at Richard’s wedding: don’t bore people.

So I decided to tell the story of the four of us. Me, Michaela, the bride, and the groom, spending four days in the wild hiking the Drakensberg Mountains.

It was a long story, but one worth telling.

The best you can hope for when giving a speech of such a personal calibre on an intimate occasion is a few genuine chuckles and an engaged room.

A good speech, I discovered, is mostly a question of cadence: knowing when to rush, when to slow down, and when to let it breathe…

Micky was sitting in the crowd, nine months pregnant.

Despite the unexpected nerves and an uninvited tear I shed towards the end of my speech, I’d call it an overall win.

Watching one of my dearest friends marry the love of his life was a privilege.

Shortly after, he and his bride headed back into that same timeless Drakensberg range for their honeymoon.

I wish them nothing but a long, happy, and healthy marriage.

Exactly two days after the wedding, my son, Ashton James Kruger, was born (now nicknamed Ash).

I like to think he waited politely for my best man speech to be delivered before making his arrival on planet Earth.

From a wedding to a birth in forty-eight hours, and through the weeks that have followed since, my mind has kept returning to the sheer physical miracle of how Ash arrived.

Watching Michaela bring our son into the world without an epidural changed how I think about human resilience, the natural rhythm of our bodies, and what we lose when modern medicine tries to hurry or bypass a process that took millions of years to perfect.

## Natural Labour and the Courage to Endure It

If you want to develop a new reverence for human resilience, watch a woman go through natural labour without an epidural.

Michaela was extraordinary.

We live in a medical environment with a heavy proclivity for scheduled interventions.

That is especially true in South African private hospitals, where elective C-sections have practically become the default path, and it is increasingly true abroad.

A recent investigation in The Times explored how Britain has become the "C-section capital of the West," with rates doubling in a decade as clinicians warn of a culture driven by fear and defensive medicine.

It is a trend that is taking hold in many countries.

Even in South Africa, because it is now so rare for women to opt for a natural delivery without an epidural, I could sense the maternity ward nurses responding to Michaela with a distinct, unspoken level of respect.

Watching what she went through solidified an opinion that might be unpopular in our hyper-medicalised era, but one I believe deeply: more women need to find the bravery to tackle natural birth, and our medical system needs to stop talking them out of it.

In our rush to sanitise, schedule, and de-risk childbirth, excessive medical intervention is often making things worse rather than better.

When we treat a healthy pregnancy as a clinical pathology waiting to go wrong, we rob mothers and babies of an evolutionary process refined over millions of years.

My opinion is understandably upsetting to many (especially mothers who have already gone the C-section route), but I’d appreciate it if you would hear me out…

Labour, I learnt, keeps its own cadence.

Contractions arrive in waves that build, peak, and release, and the rest between them is as much a part of the work as the effort itself.

It helped me to think of the nine-month pregnancy not as a waiting room where labour suddenly switches on at the forty-week mark, but as a gradual, nine-month ramp-up in intensity.

Micky’s aches and pains steadily built toward the climactic effort of bringing new life into the world.

It cannot be scripted on a hospital timetable.

It can only be listened to.

That is not to say the process was dignified and quiet.

Her screams ran right up and down the hallways of the birth ward.

I should also be honest about the supportive drugs that were in play.

This was not a story of zero pharmacological help.

Micky used short-lived relief rather than an epidural: laughing gas, and Remifentanil, an opioid that wears off in about one to two minutes and cannot be used once you start pushing.

Those tools took the edge off in the hardest stretches without flattening the process the way an epidural does.

They did not rewrite the birth.

They simply made it possible to stay inside it.

What she did not use is pethidine.

That one is a different category altogether.

It lingers in the system, crosses to the baby, and leaves newborns drowsy at the exact moment they need to be alert.

It is precisely the kind of pharmacological shortcut I am arguing against.

Preparation mattered just as much.

Micky had gone through Birthwise, and that education sat underneath every decision in the room.

It gave her a vocabulary for what her body was doing, and a spine for what she was willing to refuse.

We also had two incredible midwives, Belinda and Wikje, beside her.

At the time, I felt they were nothing short of angels standing at the forefront of bringing life into this world the way our bodies evolved to.

Wikje in particular advocated hard for less intervention.

She had the knowledge to hold the line when the medical reflex wanted to escalate.

At one point, Ashton’s heart rate dipped, and the doctor wanted to take Michaela to theatre for a C-section.

It was a serious moment and a textbook example of how quickly defensive medicine pulls the surgical trigger.

When you are on the clock in a risk-averse system, surgery is the easiest lever to pull.

But Wikje did not let that moment overwrite the plan.

She knew what Birthwise and midwifery experience had already taught Micky: a baby’s heart rate dropping during a contraction is normal and expected.

What matters is that it recovers afterwards, not that it never dips at all.

Micky herself had the awareness to notice something the monitors alone would not have explained.

She could feel that Ashton’s heart rate was correlating with the position of her body.

Shift the position, and the pattern shifted with it.

That kind of embodied knowledge is exactly what gets overwritten when a room only trusts machines and protocols.

While the medical team monitored Ash closely, Wikje and Belinda helped Micky hold her conviction all the way to the finish line.

Had a C-section become genuinely necessary to save my son or my wife, I would have been grateful the theatre was down the hall.

But Michaela was able to continue, and I am fiercely proud of what she and Ashton accomplished together.

Later, I ducked out of the labour room for a few seconds to grab some supplies from the room where she had laboured through the early stages.

Stepping into the corridor, I walked past two very calm women with relaxed smiles on their faces, waiting to be wheeled into theatre for their scheduled C-sections.

They looked at me, listened to Michaela’s scream echoing down the passage behind me, and gave me a silent, sympathetic "good luck" look.

The contrast struck me.

Choosing the natural path when a pain-free, scheduled exit door is right down the corridor requires unfathomable bravery.

Yet that bravery pays dividends that modern convenience cannot replicate.

One of our primary reasons for insisting on a natural birth is a remarkable piece of physiology in a baby’s transition to breathing.

The sheer physical compression of being pushed through the birth canal (ranked among the most painful experiences a human can endure) squeezes the amniotic fluid out of the baby’s lungs and primes their respiratory and immune systems for the outside world.

Some steps cannot be skipped, and no amount of surgical urgency can mimic them.

Ashton breathed (and cried) with full force from his first few seconds out in the world.

Having been born by C-section myself, I do suspect my own childhood asthma was the downstream cost of bypassing that natural compression (a link backed by a growing body of medical research on C-section deliveries and respiratory outcomes).

There is another benefit I almost forgot to mention, and it matters just as much.

Vaginal birth transfers a wave of the mother’s bacteria to the baby on the way out.

That first microbial handshake seeds the infant microbiome that will keep shaping digestion, immunity, and resilience for years to come.

It is one more quiet inheritance that gets skipped when birth is routed through an operating theatre.

My opinion on the matter intensifies a bit after having heard several horror stories from postpartum C-section mommies.

We rarely talk about what we steal from a mother’s neurochemistry when we turn birth into a passive surgical procedure.

Enduring and conquering the intensity of natural labour triggers a profound, evolutionary cascade of hormones (oxytocin, endorphins, and adrenaline) designed to wire a mother’s brain for immediate bonding and resilience.

Crossing that finish line under her own power gives a woman an unshakeable sense of agency.

Bypassing that natural rhythm through unnecessary intervention leaves too many women recovering from major abdominal surgery while feeling disconnected from the birth itself, making them far more vulnerable to postpartum depression.

There is also the sheer cocktail of drugs pumped into a woman’s body during heavily medicated and surgical deliveries.

Those anaesthetics, synthetic hormones, and heavy painkillers do not stay isolated to the mother; they cross over to the child.

You see it in how many babies come out completely loopy, groggy, and sluggish in their first hours of life, fighting through a chemical fog at the exact moment they are meant to be alert, latching, and bonding with their mothers.

When medical intervention becomes the default rather than the rescue mechanism, it creates the very complications it claims to prevent.

Fear-based, defensive obstetrics interrupts the body’s natural rhythm, stalls labour, induces panic, and manufactures emergencies that end on an operating table.

And the pharmacological conveyor belt does not stop once the baby is delivered.

Even after a natural birth, the reflex toward chemical shortcuts kept showing up.

Doctors were quite eager to give Ash antibiotics, including antibiotic eyedrops, almost as a matter of course.

Why?

Like, genuinely, why?

A healthy newborn who had just completed the whole evolutionary gauntlet somehow still needed a prophylactic chemical send-off in his eyes?

We experienced that same medical reflex firsthand with Michaela too.

When we voiced a concern that her breast milk had not fully come in yet, the immediate response was a proposal for milk-inducing hormone medication, offered with almost no discussion about trusting her body’s timing or the negative side effects of artificially forcing lactation.

I am grateful we held off and researched it ourselves.

For one, it is completely normal for mature milk to take a few days to arrive.

More importantly, that delay is evolutionary by design: what arrives first is a concentrated, nutrient-dense colostrum that seeds the foundation of a baby’s gut microbiome.

That initial window before full milk production also triggers the newborn’s ketone production and ketosis mechanisms, switching on vital metabolic survival pathways for later in life.

Rushing to medicate the mother or throwing a baby straight onto formula out of impatience robs them of that foundational biological calibration.

The heavy rotation of postoperative drugs, painkillers, and sedatives that follow in the wake of surgical intervention dulls a mother’s physical and mental resilience right when she needs her vitality most.

By blunting her body's natural hormonal recovery and replacing it with a chemical one, the process leaves mothers emotionally flatlined and significantly more susceptible to postpartum psychological complications and depression.

At its core, this comes down to how we view doing hard things in life.

Think about the bravery it takes to run a marathon and the profound sense of achievement that washes over you when you cross that finish line.

Nobody runs 42 kilometres because it is painless or convenient; you do it because enduring the agony and pushing through the wall transforms you.

You would never tell a marathon runner to take a taxi to the finish line just to spare them the sweat and discomfort.

We need to start training mothers to think about childbirth the same way: to prepare their minds and bodies to train for labour, to embrace the grit it demands, and to be immensely proud of themselves when they conquer it naturally.

Is there lots of screaming? Hell yes.

Should women be afraid of it?

I think you are allowed to be afraid.

Fear is honest.

What I want more of is a healthy respect for that fear, rather than a medical culture that treats fear as a reason to override the process entirely.

Respect the intensity.

Respect the risk.

Then prepare for it, instead of fleeing it by default.

I imagine the entire maternity ward at Netcare Pretoria East Hospital must have been slightly on edge for a few hours while Michaela fought through her 7.5-hour battle, or perhaps they are used to it.

Either way, she was unfathomably brave.

It is worth mentioning that being there also gave me a deep appreciation for the people who keep a maternity ward running around the clock.

Doctors, nurses, and midwives moved through a relentless 24/7 system of monitoring, decisions, handovers, and care.

Shift after shift, handover after handover, a human system built to hold its rhythm through day and night.

It made me appreciate not just the practice of the operations, but the orchestration of the humans behind it.

## Three Billion Heartbeats

I am still weighing the good and the bad of the hospital experience as a whole.

Modern hospitals are strange, clinical places to welcome a new human being, and I wish their designers cared a little more about warmth, softer lighting, and putting a living plant or two in the corner of the room.

Still, I am not blind to the privilege of the safety net around us.

Across the animal kingdom, most mammals get roughly one billion heartbeats in a lifetime, whether they burn through them in two years like a mouse or stretch them across decades like a whale.

Humans are the great exception, routinely reaching three billion heartbeats.

We earned those extra two billion beats through centuries of patient human innovation in sanitation, science, and genuine emergency medicine.

The mistake of modern obstetrics is forgetting when to use that power and when to stand down.

True medicine is there to rescue a life when nature falters, not to script a life before it has even begun.

Watching Michaela bring our son into the world taught me that life itself demands that same respect.

You cannot rush a four-day hike through the Drakensberg, you cannot skip the hard miles of a marathon, and you should not shortcut the ancient, brutal, and extraordinary work of birth.

Ashton James Kruger is here now, healthy, alert, and right at the beginning of his three billion heartbeats.

So far, Ash is doing well.

He feeds, he settles, he stares at the world like he is still deciding what to make of it.

That is enough for now.

But newborns are hard, and Micky’s marathon has not ended.

Labour was a real milestone. It mattered. It changed her.

It just was not the end of the hard part.

What comes after is night feeds, cluster feeds, and days that blur into each other.

Sleep is scarce.

You take whatever rest you can get and keep going.

I am watching Michaela do that with the same resolve she had in the labour room, only now the work is mostly invisible.

Feeding him at 3am. Settling him again at 4. Trying to rest for twenty minutes and hoping he lets you.

It is ordinary, relentless, and easy to underestimate from the outside.

I am grateful Ash is here and doing well.

I am grateful Micky had the preparation, the midwives, and the conviction to hold the line when the system wanted to hurry.

And I am grateful we get to be this tired for a reason this good.

Watching his mother fight for every single second of his arrival was among the greatest privileges of my life.

The next part is quieter. Staying present. Stay patient… enjoying these moments and reflecting on a time that will *inevitably* pass by in the blink of an eye.
