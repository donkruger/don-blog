There are two layers to knowing. The first is superficial. You can say the words of the thing you know to be true but seldom take it to heart.

The second is when your life is an embodiment of what you know to be true. It is where the truth is inhaled, embraced and experienced with certainty through a million different angles.

Most of what I know still sits in the first layer. The trick is noticing which of the two you are actually living in. Writing turns out to be one of the few honest ways to check.

I recently realised I’m not entirely fond of LinkedIn (and the social media drug in general). But my favourite part of it in recent years has been reading personal essays (such as those from Carel & Charles).

In this age of AI and em dashes I find myself longing for the odd typo and grammatical error.

There’s a premium to be paid for when that unique imperfect human tone comes through in your writing. In Charles’ recent Savage Take he wrote “the discipline of writing doesn't just document your thinking, it builds it”.

I’ve known writing is a healthy mental exercise for a long time. Michaela journals avidly and often recommends I do the same.

So I’ve peeled back a few of the secondary layers of ‘knowing’ in recent months. That unlocked the time and emotional capacity to share here.

## Baby on the way

We’re giving away our TV and deleted social media apps from our phones.

It’s no secret that mobile phone addiction and cheap dopamine is the very much a modern struggle and in many ways a modern devil. I decided I’d rather be a present father, than a distracted one.

This decision is less of mind and more of environment. I’m a believer the environment always wins the war. So no more TV… at least for the next few foundational years.

## Shackles of light

During our daily stroll to Seattle Coffee this morning Michaela asked me to describe what it feels like cutting out social media. I said it feels like severing shackles that were tied to my eyeballs via beams of light. The kind of shackles you only realise were there after a long time of them being severed.

A highly sophisticated and sinister kind of modern shackle if you think about it.

What grows back in its place once severed is boredom.

Boredom is a powerful commodity, the ultimate catalyst for creativity.

Granting your mind the time and space to get bored is kind of the point and the goal.

I assumed it would send me back to the books I had been meaning to finish. But it didn’t.

What the boredom actually surfaced was an appetite to create and restore.

It started with ‘life ops’, admin and errands rather than long form entertainment.

For example, my mother’s garage (piled to the brim with boxes of storage with zero space for a car) was a long-forgotten system in her life that needed tackling.

Various small life nitty gritties all fall into some state of entropy over time that actually necessitate a certain level of attention and awareness.

So we’re tackling them one system at a time.

Boredom also sharpens your eye. When there is nothing pulling at your attention you start noticing the things you would otherwise have walked straight past.

The small details become a kind of currency.

I’m referring to the things that go unnoticed because nobody is standing still long enough to notice them.

## One such thing is the Peyote Cactus…

I would consider myself a bit of a psychedelic nerd (moreso in the documentary and late-night reading sense than anything else).

I have known what a Peyote looks like for a long time, without ever expecting to meet one. Then last Saturday at the Hazelwood flea market, between a table of freshly squeezed juice and somebody’s tray of succulents, there it was. A baby Peyote the size of a marble, sitting in a plastic pot completely unsuspecting.

A Peyote typically takes anywhere from 10-30 years to mature and flower and may grow no larger than a tiny apple. It houses over 50 psychoactive alkaloids, the most prominent of which is Mescaline.

Plucking one from the ground is like decapitating an endangered Bonsai.

The Peyote has been used by the Native Americans in spiritual ceremonies for millennia (and they are also not very fond of people picking them from the ground).

I must say it scores by no means among the prettiest cacti. It looks like the cacti equivalent of that tiny little immortal creature thingamajig they call a Tardigrade.

Anyway… I bought it for R80. Which is to say I procured a Schedule 1 narcotic.

Well technically South African law only recognises it crossing the barrier from cactus to Schedule 1 illegal substance when one harvests it with intent for consumption (Schedule 1 being on the level of Crystal Meth or Heroin).

As long as the plant remains untouched on my balcony, its legal status is no matter of concern.

In a world optimized for instant dopamine, this little marble-sized plant is such a fitting symbol of defiance. And it comes of maturity more or less around the age I am approaching – the tender age of 30.

My resonation with the Peyote is a sort of kinship as you can imagine.

The Peyote serves as a subtle reminder to appreciate those compounding things that can only be achieved on the scale of decades.

## Simple Subtractions

My stance here is that truly subtracting the virtual distractions is a modern superpower if you give it enough time to calibrate.

Subtracting something you resent can indeed teach you a lot. But more interestingly I found subtracting something you find pleasure in can grant you awareness.

I find it fascinating to live through the results of these subtractions… a cleared out garage, longer conversations with Michaela and generally enhanced clarity of thought.

Less is very often more…especially in this world of abundance.

## Closing out my 20s

So, I write to close out the last few months of my twenties with profound gratitude. I am thankful for the extraordinary privilege of a soon-to-be-born son, for Michaela, and for the honour of standing as best man at my dearest friend Richard’s wedding this October.

Most of all, I am grateful for the mistakes, the successes, and the extraordinary challenges I have lived through in these defining years.

Cheers to reaching that second layer of knowing. Cheers to boredom… to having the wisdom to cut out the distractions that numb us, and the courage to fully embrace the things that make us more human.

Cheers to the Peyote flower and the kinship I feel with its bloom. Here’s to more life, more gratitude, and hopefully, the privilege of another thirty years to experience the beauties of the things that compound over decades.
